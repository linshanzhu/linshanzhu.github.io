Teaching & Mentoring

Teaching Philosophy

I believe that the most meaningful measure of learning is whether students can apply fundamental principles to unfamiliar problems. My teaching emphasizes conceptual understanding, quantitative reasoning, experimental design, and independent problem-solving rather than memorization alone.

I combine step-by-step instruction with active learning, real-world research examples, and modern visualization tools. In bioengineering courses, I connect foundational concepts to authentic scientific challenges, such as designing molecular biosensors, interpreting cellular signaling dynamics, or evaluating engineered therapeutic systems.

My goal is for students to develop the confidence and intellectual independence to ask meaningful questions, critically evaluate evidence, and defend their scientific reasoning.

Mentoring is a central part of my academic work. I have worked with trainees at different stages of their education, providing individualized guidance in experimental design, scientific reasoning, data analysis, research communication, and career development.

Selected Mentoring Outcomes

  • NIH F31 Fellowship: A Ph.D. trainee I mentored successfully obtained an NIH F31 fellowship.
  • Conference Presentations: Trainees have presented their research at the Biomedical Engineering Society (BMES) Annual Meeting.
  • Peer-Reviewed Publication: An undergraduate researcher I mentored contributed to a peer-reviewed publication.
  • Career Development: An undergraduate trainee I supervised subsequently matriculated into medical school.

My mentoring philosophy centers on progressive independence. I encourage trainees to formulate meaningful research questions, design rigorous experiments, interpret data critically, and communicate their findings clearly. I also emphasize accessible guidance, constructive feedback, and a supportive environment that maintains high scientific standards.

Educational Goals

I aim to prepare students not only to understand existing biological knowledge but also to question assumptions, integrate ideas across disciplines, and develop original approaches to scientific challenges.

Ultimately, I define successful teaching by whether students can think independently, solve unfamiliar problems, and defend their reasoning with evidence.
